Mechanical design and software engineering are separate subjects in traditional engineering education. While design work is entirely digital today, designers are predominantly _users_ of software, not developers. Still, they produce digital artifacts through complex workflows, iterating and refining them throughout the product development process. For software engineers coming to mechanical design as outsiders, many challenges – managing revisions, validating design requirements – are familiar, but the tools for addressing them seem to be missing: encapsulation, version control, or continuous integration are difficult to impose on point-and-click CAD systems. Defining parts _as code_ turns the design itself into a software artifact and makes it possible to bring all those methods to mechanical design.

Representing CAD as code is not a new idea. Its modern incarnation started with the widespread availability of consumer 3D printers, making it possible to express design intent in a modern programming language and to share CAD models in the same way as code. The decisive change, however, has been the advent of large language models (LLMs). By transforming the entire engineering design process, they have elevated code-based CAD to a first-class place in the modern engineer's toolbox. Representing CAD as code does more than accelerate the _generation_ of a design: it is a prerequisite for auditing and reasoning about an AI-generated design, because it encodes intent, not just geometry and topology. Ultimately, it enables end-to-end engineering pipelines that make the entire workflow – from design through simulation – accessible to automation and generative AI.

Against this backdrop, it was a fortunate coincidence that I had the opportunity in early 2026 to redesign _Programming of CAx Systems_, a course in the Computational Engineering master’s program at Munich University of Applied Sciences (HM). Whereas the previous version focused on developing and automating CAD systems with a graphical user interface (GUI), the redesigned course teaches code-first CAD and its applications. This textbook is the result of the first offering of the redesigned course in the summer semester of 2026.

My own path into CAD was not through mechanical design. By training, I'm a physicist, not an engineer, and I came to the field through modelling, simulation, and optimization. Experienced designers will no doubt find places in this book where this background shows. The students in my course, on the other hand, have a strong foundation in mechanical design but typically have limited programming experience and even less experience in software engineering. This book is therefore intended for two groups of learners: those with design experience but little software engineering experience, and those with software experience but little knowledge of mechanical design.

This book covers a subject that is being transformed by generative AI. Writing it was also an opportunity for me to experiment with the rapidly improving ability of LLMs to generate text on technical topics. I wrote this preface by forming each sentence in my head and then typing each letter on a physical keyboard. But all subsequent chapters were generated by a large language model based on my prompts, with my lecture materials as context. I tried to avoid manual editing, revising the text through further prompts instead. Perhaps I wrote more words in prompts than this book contains. Does that make it an AI-generated book? Technically, yes. But I still consider it human-authored: it reflects my expertise and my experience teaching the course.

Because all the software this book builds upon is open source, it felt appropriate to make the book truly open source, too. So not only is the content released under a permissive license, but the template and build pipeline, including the full version history, are openly available as well. As with open-source _software_, if you find a mistake or other issue in the book, you are encouraged to open an issue in the book's repository.[^repo] Because the book accompanies an annual course at HM, I plan to update it between course offerings and to include improvements and corrections in each new release.
